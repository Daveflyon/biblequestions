Women in trousers and men in skirts: what about Deuteronomy 22:5?

The Question

"Christians, why is it that your women wear men's clothing and even some of the men also wear women's attire?"

Thank you for raising this. I appreciate that it comes from a desire to honour Scripture and to preserve the God-given distinction between men and women. That is a good and important concern and I do not want to dismiss it.

In short: Deuteronomy 22:5 is a real command and it still matters. It forbids taking on the identity of the other sex. It does not name trousers. Women's trousers are designed and recognised as women's clothing so a woman wearing them keeps the verse. A man who deliberately presents himself as a woman does not. Along the way this answer takes the strongest objections head on. Numbers in square brackets point to the sources at the end.

1. What Deuteronomy 22:5 Says

The verse often referred to is Deuteronomy 22:5:

"A woman must not wear men's clothing, nor a man wear women's clothing, for the LORD your God detests anyone who does this."

Deuteronomy 22:5 (NIV)[1]

At first glance it can seem straightforward. But the question is what "men's clothing" and "women's clothing" meant in ancient Israel and how that principle applies today.

The Hebrew is more striking than most English versions show. The verse names two different items. What a woman must not put on is keli geber. Keli is a wide word. The standard lexicon lists tools, vessels, weapons and ornaments among its senses and also apparel, giving this very verse as its example of that last sense[3]. Geber means a man with the emphasis on strength and is a word often used of a warrior[3]. It always means an adult male[2]. What a man must not put on is simlat ishah, a woman's cloak or outer garment[2]. Commentators agree that the verse bans men and women taking up what belongs to the other sex[2]. English versions render keli geber as "man's apparel" or "article proper to a man" and so on[4] so the exact reach of the first half is debated. At the least the verse is about clothing and some scholars think it reaches further.

"Doesn't 'detests' mean this is a very serious command?"

Yes. The Hebrew word is to'evah. The lexicon gives it a ritual sense (idolatry and unclean food for example) and an ethical sense covering lying and dishonest scales and sexual immorality[3]. The same word is used of dishonest dealing (Deuteronomy 25:16)[1]. It tells us God does not accept the act. It does not by itself tell us whether a law is moral or ceremonial since the word covers both[2][3] so we look at what the law rests on. God takes this seriously and so should we. The question is not whether the verse matters but what exactly it forbids.

2. Why God Gave It

The reason goes back to the first chapter of the Bible:

"So God created mankind in his own image, in the image of God he created them; male and female he created them."

Genesis 1:27 (NIV)[1]

Male and female are part of God's good design. Deuteronomy 22:5 protects that design in everyday life. The Bible does not spell out every purpose behind the verse and Jewish tradition gives several readings. Rashi read it as a ban on disguise used for adultery[5] and the Talmud (Nazir 59a) reads it in a similar way[2]. Maimonides linked it to idolatrous rites although some scholars find that unlikely because Deuteronomy usually says when a practice belongs to foreign worship[2]. Philo tied it to manliness and the order of society[2]. These readings differ in reach. The disguise reading is narrow and would forbid only cross-dressing that deceives. The creation reading is broad and would forbid dress that deliberately blurs the distinction. This answer follows the broad reading because Jesus cites Genesis 1:27 when teaching on marriage (Matthew 19:4) and Paul roots the distinction between man and woman in creation (1 Corinthians 11:8–12)[1]. That is a judgement about the verse's purpose and not a statement of its text. What the text makes clear is that God cares about the distinction and does not want it blurred.

"Isn't this a moral command? Why treat it differently from the rules on wool and linen?"

We should not. Christians have traditionally divided the law of Moses into moral, ceremonial and judicial parts. The moral law binds everyone while the ceremonial laws pointed to Christ and have been fulfilled in him[6]. A careful reader will notice that the neighbouring laws also keep things apart: two kinds of seed in a vineyard (22:9), an ox and a donkey yoked together (22:10) and wool and linen woven together (22:11). Tassels on the corners of a cloak follow (22:12). Christians have traditionally seen these as marks of Israel's separate life and so part of what Jesus fulfilled. So why does 22:5 carry over? Because the New Testament carries over its ground. Jesus cites Genesis 1:27 when teaching on marriage (Matthew 19:4) and Paul roots the distinction between man and woman in creation (1 Corinthians 11:8–12)[1]. Nothing like that is said of seed or fabric. That is my reason for placing 22:5 with the moral law. Not every Christian sorts individual laws in exactly the same way so I give my reason and do not simply assert it. Jesus said:

"Do not think that I have come to abolish the Law or the Prophets; I have not come to abolish them but to fulfill them."

Matthew 5:17 (NIV)[1]

Jesus goes on to say that not the smallest letter will disappear from the Law until everything is accomplished (Matthew 5:18) and he warns against setting aside even the least of its commands and teaching others to do the same (Matthew 5:19)[1]. A stricter reader will take that seriously and so should we. It is why this answer does not treat the verse as optional. How the Law is fulfilled in Christ is a larger question than this page can settle. The point here is narrower: the verse stands as a principle for us and the question is what it asks of us.

3. The Original Setting

Deuteronomy 22:5 appears among laws covering everyday life, safety, property and worship. Men and women both commonly wore a tunic with an outer cloak[7] so trousers were not the everyday marker of male identity there as they often are in modern Britain. Women's dress probably differed in details such as length and sleeves although the evidence is limited and part of it is inferred from this very verse[7].

So the verse was not saying "Women must never wear trousers" because trousers were not the issue. It was addressing the wearing of clothing clearly associated with the opposite sex. It was likely concerned with preserving sexual distinction and possibly with distancing Israel from practices connected with surrounding religions.

4. What Jesus and Mary Wore

Jesus wore a robe. Women of the time wore robes too. The Gospels tell us Jesus wore a tunic and an outer cloak:

"When the soldiers crucified Jesus, they took his clothes, dividing them into four shares, one for each of them, with the undergarment remaining. This garment was seamless, woven in one piece from top to bottom."

John 19:23 (NIV)[1]

Scripture does not describe what Mary wore but women of that time wore the same basic layers as men[7]. The difference was probably in details such as length and sleeves and not in the basic category of clothing[7]. A robe was not automatically "men's clothing" simply because men wore robes.

That helps us with the question of trousers today. A garment is not necessarily male simply because men also wear that general type of clothing. The important question is whether the particular garment is designed and recognised as clothing for one's own sex.

"Doesn't that just show that men's and women's robes differed so the distinction existed?"

Yes it did and that is the point. The distinction mattered and the people of the day could read it. As far as we can tell they read it in details such as length and sleeves and not in the category of garment[7]. Deuteronomy 22:5 asks us to keep a distinction our own culture can read. It does not hand us a list of approved shapes.

5. How Men's and Women's Trousers Differ

Modern men's and women's trousers are not simply the same garment with a different label. They are usually cut differently for different bodies and purposes. This is not Bible but it answers one question: how does our culture read the garment? The clothing trade makes and sells these as different garments.

These are typical patterns. Fit varies by maker and style. The pocket difference is documented: one analysis found women's jeans pockets 48% shorter and 6.5% narrower than men's[8].

These differences show that women's trousers are designed as women's clothing. A woman wearing modest, feminine-cut trousers is not wearing "men's clothing"; she is wearing clothing made for women.

Where a garment is genuinely unisex the question becomes one of intent and presentation, not cut alone. What counts as "men's" or "women's" clothing is settled by what a culture recognises (section 6 gives examples).

"But women wore dresses for centuries. Isn't trousers on women just a recent rebellion?"

Trousers became common for British women during the Second World War as factory work and clothing rationing made them practical. One report put women's trouser sales in the summer of 1944 at five times the previous year. Restrictions in schools and workplaces then eased gradually from the 1960s[8]. So everyday acceptance is within living memory and some see it as part of a wider cultural rebellion. That is a fair point and I do not dismiss it. But how recent a custom is cannot settle the question. What settles it is whether the garment presents a woman as a man. Today nobody mistakes a woman in women's trousers for a man and the garment is made and sold as women's clothing. In South Asia women have worn the shalwar, a loose trouser, for centuries[9]. A custom can be new in one place and old in another so the verse's own test has to decide: is this being used to present as the other sex?

6. What About Men Wearing Women's Clothing?

The question raises this too and it deserves a straight answer. A man who deliberately dresses to present himself as a woman is doing what Deuteronomy 22:5 describes. The Bible does not approve of that.

"Isn't a man in a skirt exactly what the verse forbids?"

Not when the culture reads the garment as acceptable for men. A Scotsman in a kilt is wearing a skirt-like garment yet nobody in Scotland would say he is dressed as a woman. The kilt is an accepted exception to the Western rule that skirts are not men's wear[10]. Skirt-like garments are ordinary dress for men in many other places. In Fiji the sulu is worn by men and women. In Samoa the lavalava is worn by men and women with different patterns. In Myanmar the longyi reaches mid-calf for men and the ankles for women[10]. In each place people read who the garment belongs to from its style and not from the bare fact that it is a skirt. You do not need to know these cultures to see the point: the people of a place decide what counts as men's or women's clothing. What Deuteronomy 22:5 forbids is not a shape of garment but a man using dress to present himself as a woman.

Saying it plainly does not mean saying it harshly. Many people who wrestle with their sense of being male or female carry real pain. The church is called to hold to God's design and to welcome every person with the same grace that welcomed us. Truth and love belong together.

7. How the New Testament Carries the Principle

The New Testament does not repeat Deuteronomy 22:5 but it keeps the same principle. Paul shows how it works in practice:

"Judge for yourselves: Is it proper for a woman to pray to God with her head uncovered? Does not the very nature of things teach you that if a man has long hair, it is a disgrace to him, but that if a woman has long hair, it is her glory? For long hair is given to her as a covering."

1 Corinthians 11:13–15 (NIV)[1]

"Doesn't Paul say 'the very nature of things'? Isn't that fixed and not cultural?"

Paul does appeal to nature and I agree that the distinction between male and female is fixed by God. What can differ is how a culture marks it. Paul closes the passage by pointing to the practice of the churches (verse 16)[1]. Some markers plainly vary. What counts as long hair differs between cultures and few would insist on Israelite robes today.

Christians read the details of this passage in different ways. The main thread is clear though. Paul keeps the distinction between men and women and expects it to be visible. The principle stays fixed. How the outward marker is read depends on the setting.

8. What About Modesty?

Many Christians who hold the stricter view care less about the Hebrew than about modesty and about not conforming to the pattern of this world (Romans 12:2). That deserves respect.

Even if we do not apply Deuteronomy 22:5 as a ban on trousers it still carries a principle worth taking seriously: God cares about how we present ourselves and He does not call us to blur or deny the distinction between male and female. For Christians today that principle can be applied through modesty, appropriateness and sincerity of heart. The New Testament encourages believers to dress modestly and sensibly with an emphasis on inner character rather than outward show (1 Timothy 2:9–10; 1 Peter 3:3–4).

Modesty is about cut and heart and not about category. Tight trousers can be immodest. So can a short skirt. A loose modest pair of trousers can be more modest than a short dress. The right question is not "trousers or skirt?" but "does this honour God and the people around me?"

"Doesn't Romans 12:2 tell us not to copy the world? Isn't following fashion exactly that?"

It does tell us that and the call is worth hearing:

"Do not conform to the pattern of this world, but be transformed by the renewing of your mind."

Romans 12:2 (NIV)[1]

Notice what Paul sets against conforming. It is not a different cut of cloth. It is the renewing of the mind. The pattern of this world is a way of thinking and living: vanity, display, rivalry and living as though God's design did not matter. A woman can wear a long skirt out of pride. Another can wear trousers out of plain practicality and humble faith. So the question to ask is not "what does the world wear?" but "what is driving my choice?"

If we dress to draw attention or to compete or to blur what God has made then we are conforming. If we dress to serve or to work or to be modest and to honour God then we are not conforming whatever the garment. Those who choose stricter dress out of a desire to stand apart for Christ are living out this verse in their own way and I honour that. The verse does not tell us which garment God approves. It tells us what to look for in our own hearts.

9. A Simple Test

Three questions help anyone apply the verse:

  1. Is it made for and recognised as clothing for my sex in my culture?
  2. Am I wearing it to present myself as the other sex?
  3. Is it modest and does it honour God?

If the answers are yes then no then yes the garment keeps both the verse and the heart behind it.

10. Where Sincere Christians Differ

Some Christians believe trousers remain culturally associated with men and therefore should not be worn by women. Others believe trousers are now ordinary women's clothing and that the heart of the verse is about deliberately presenting oneself as the opposite sex.

Both positions take the Bible seriously. The difference lies in how we move from an ancient cultural setting to our own. Paul gives clear guidance for exactly this kind of difference:

"Accept the one whose faith is weak, without quarreling over disputable matters."

Romans 14:1 (NIV)[1]

"Doesn't Romans 14 just let everyone do what they like?"

No. Paul asks each person to be "fully convinced in their own mind" (Romans 14:5)[1] and to act from conviction before God. He asks the stricter not to condemn the freer and the freer not to flaunt their freedom:

"Therefore let us stop passing judgment on one another. Instead, make up your mind not to put any stumbling block or obstacle in the way of a brother or sister."

Romans 14:13 (NIV)[1]

Romans 14 covers disputable matters only. It does not tell us which matters those are so placing women's trousers there is a judgement I make from the reasons above and not a verse I can point to. A man deliberately presenting himself as a woman does not belong there because Deuteronomy 22:5 speaks to it directly.

My own conclusion is that a woman may wear modest, feminine-cut trousers without contradicting Deuteronomy 22:5. The verse does not name trousers and trousers are no longer a clear marker of male identity. I hold this with humility because it rests on a principle (Genesis 1:27, Matthew 19:4 and 1 Corinthians 11) and on how our culture reads the garment and not on a verse that names trousers. At the same time I respect the conviction of those who choose a different practice for themselves or their families.

What matters most is that we seek to honour God, respect one another and avoid turning a matter of application into a test of someone's love for the Lord. God looks first at the heart: "People look at the outward appearance, but the LORD looks at the heart" (1 Samuel 16:7)[1]. That does not make outward things unimportant as Deuteronomy 22:5 itself shows. It tells us where to begin.

11. The Clothing That Matters Most

Whatever we decide about trousers or skirts there is a garment every follower of Jesus is asked to wear. Paul puts it this way:

"So in Christ Jesus you are all children of God through faith, for all of you who were baptized into Christ have clothed yourselves with Christ."

Galatians 3:26–27 (NIV)[1]

That clothing is not earned by any cut of cloth. It is given by grace to every man and woman who trusts Him. Paul goes on to say that in Christ there is no Jew or Gentile and no slave or free and that "you are all one in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:28)[1]. He is speaking of how we stand before God and belong to one another: every one of us comes to Christ on the same footing. He is not undoing creation. The same Paul still writes of men and women honouring the distinction (1 Corinthians 11). So we hold both together: one family in Christ and the good design of male and female.

And the clothing we are asked to put on each day is the character of Christ himself:

"Therefore, as God's chosen people, holy and dearly loved, clothe yourselves with compassion, kindness, humility, gentleness and patience."

Colossians 3:12 (NIV)[1]

Get that right and the questions about trousers and skirts find their proper place. If you still have questions about this or anything else in Scripture please send them in. Every one is read.

How to Say It Simply

Plain Response

"Deuteronomy 22:5 is a real command about a man or woman taking on the identity of the other sex. God made us male and female (Genesis 1:27) and He wants that kept clear. It is a moral principle so it still applies but it does not name trousers. Jesus and the people of His day wore robes so a robe was not 'men's clothing' just because men wore it. Today women's trousers are cut, made and sold as women's clothing just as the Scottish kilt is recognised as a man's. So a woman in women's trousers keeps the verse. A man dressing to present as a woman does not. Where Christians differ on the details Romans 14 tells us to accept one another and not judge. And the clothing that matters most is Christ himself (Galatians 3:27)."
