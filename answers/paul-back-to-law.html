Salvation

Did Paul go back on grace or was he appeasing when he joined a vow in Acts 21:19–26?

The Question

In Acts 21:19–26, Paul agrees to "take them and be purified with them, and pay their expenses so that they may shave their heads" (Acts 21:24). This act appears to show that Paul went back to keeping the law. Is this not the same Paul who taught us grace and that the law cannot save you (Galatians 2:16)? Yet when push came to shove, he reverted to keeping the law. Well then, why did Paul go back on his own grace teaching? If not, how do you explain this from sound biblical basis?

Thank you for raising this. It is a fair question that deserves a careful answer. Acts 21:19–26 can look like a U-turn, so it is worth reading the passage below slowly and in order.

In short: No. Paul did not go back on grace. The answer turns on a distinction. Being justified before God never comes through the works of the law. A Jewish practice is a different matter. In Acts 21:19–26 Paul took part in a Jewish custom to reassure Jewish believers. He did not make that custom a way to be right with God. The gospel he taught stayed the same. The plan also backfired. The chapter ends with Paul arrested in the temple. Along the way this answer takes the hardest objections head on. Numbers in square brackets point to the sources at the end.

Nor was he appeasing. Appeasement gives up the principle to make the pressure stop. Accommodation keeps the principle and gives up only a practice, freely, for the sake of others. Paul did the second. Sections 2 and 5 set out the difference.

19When he had greeted them, he told in detail those things which God had done among the Gentiles through his ministry. 20And when they heard it, they glorified the Lord. And they said to him, "You see, brother, how many myriads of Jews there are who have believed, and they are all zealous for the law; 21but they have been informed about you that you teach all the Jews who are among the Gentiles to forsake Moses, saying that they ought not to circumcise their children nor to walk according to the customs. 22What then? The assembly must certainly meet, for they will hear that you have come. 23Therefore do what we tell you: We have four men who have taken a vow. 24Take them and be purified with them, and pay their expenses so that they may shave their heads, and that all may know that those things of which they were informed concerning you are nothing, but that you yourself also walk orderly and keep the law. 25But concerning the Gentiles who believe, we have written and decided that they should observe no such thing, except that they should keep themselves from things offered to idols, from blood, from things strangled, and from sexual immorality." 26Then Paul took the men, and the next day, having been purified with them, entered the temple to announce the expiration of the days of purification, at which time an offering should be made for each one of them.

Acts 21:19–26[1]

In plain words: Paul tells the leaders what God had done among the Gentiles (verse 19). The leaders in Jerusalem tell Paul that many Jewish believers are still zealous for the law (verse 20). They have heard a rumour that Paul teaches Jews living among the Gentiles to abandon Moses (verse 21). Their answer is that Paul should join four men who have taken a vow, pay their costs and be purified with them, so that everyone can see the rumour is false (verses 22 to 24). The decision for Gentile believers stands (verse 25). Paul agrees and does it (verse 26).

This decision goes back to Acts 15. The church wrote that it seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to the leaders who wrote it, "to lay upon you no greater burden than these necessary things" (Acts 15:28)[1]. Acts 21:19–26 does not cancel that letter. It repeats it. The request is about Paul and about Jewish believers who keep their customs, not about Gentiles who are asked to be circumcised. Osborne says the letter's aim was practical: "The goal is not to hamstring the Gentiles but to promote harmony between Jews and Gentiles in the church."[2]

Acts 18:18 tells us Paul had taken a vow earlier, at Cenchrea: "for he had taken a vow."[1] The text does not name that vow. In Acts 21:19–26 four men had a vow on them. Paul was asked to purify himself with them and to pay their expenses (Acts 21:23–24).[1]

Numbers 6 describes the Nazirite vow. A person kept from wine. No razor came upon the head during the days of the vow:

"All the days of the vow of his separation no razor shall come upon his head."

Numbers 6:5[1]

When the days were fulfilled, the vow ended with offerings: a male lamb for a burnt offering, a ewe lamb for a sin offering and a ram for a peace offering, with unleavened bread and grain and drink offerings (Numbers 6:13–15)[1].

Acts 21:19–26 does not say Paul took a vow himself. It says he was to purify himself with the four men and pay their expenses, which was a costly gesture. Gangel says the request was not "in the vow itself". It was "in the purification ceremony and in the payment of their expenses"[3]. Osborne says the suggestion aimed "to show the Jews of Jerusalem that Paul is still faithful to his Jewish tradition"[2]. The Bible Knowledge Background Commentary says the act of purification "was clearly intended to assure those in Jerusalem that Paul was not opposed to the Law"[4].

Stott puts the distinction plainly. Paul and James were "already agreed doctrinally (that salvation was by grace in Christ through faith)". The dispute concerned "culture, ceremony and tradition"[5]. The customs were kept. They were never treated as the way to be saved.

Acts 21:26 records the act itself. Paul "entered the temple to announce the expiration of the days of purification, at which time an offering should be made for each one of them."[1]

"Isn't this compromise?"

It can look like it. Osborne is candid: "Whether this was a wise decision has long been debated, for it certainly did backfire."[2]. Stott answers the charge directly. The solution Paul and James reached was "a concession in the area of practice", not the surrender of a doctrinal or moral principle[5]. Gangel is more sympathetic: "Perhaps the criticism is unjust since they faced a real problem"[3]. The honest answer has two parts. First, the gospel was not on the table in Acts 21:19–26. The Gentile question had been settled in Acts 15. The elders repeat that decision in verse 25. Second, Paul gave way on a custom. The result was trouble, not reconciliation. The plan ended in a riot in which Paul was seized on a charge that he had brought Greeks into the temple.[1] The chapter does not hide that cost. Readers differ on whether this was a mistake or a sincere act of love that went wrong.

"Isn't this appeasement?"

Appeasement gives way to a demand so that the pressure stops. The principle goes with it. Accommodation adapts a practice freely for another's good and keeps the principle. Paul drew that line himself. When false brethren pressed for Titus to be circumcised, he did not yield "even for an hour" (Galatians 2:5).[1] In Acts 21 the pressure is not on Paul's principle. The elders tell him what to do about the rumours (Acts 21:23). The decree protecting Gentiles is restated (Acts 21:25).[1] The objection is fair in one respect. Giving way did not bring calm. Luke records the riot that followed (Acts 21:27–30).[1] But a bad outcome does not show that the act was a surrender. Paul had counted the cost. He said he was "ready not only to be bound, but also to die at Jerusalem for the name of the Lord Jesus" (Acts 21:13).[1]

What did Jesus keep when he paid a tax he did not owe? Read Matthew 17:24–27 before you answer.

Jesus stated the principle first: "Then the sons are free" (Matthew 17:26).[1] He then paid, "lest we offend them" (Matthew 17:27).[1] He gave up the custom for others while keeping his freedom. Peter, by contrast, withdrew from Gentile believers "fearing those who were of the circumcision" (Galatians 2:12).[1] He gave way to fear. The truth of the gospel was at stake (Galatians 2:14).[1]

So the difference is this. Appeasement gives up the principle to make the pressure stop. Accommodation keeps the principle and gives up only the practice, freely, for the good of others. The test is what is still standing once the pressure has passed. In Jesus's case, his freedom. In Acts 21, the decree that protects Gentiles (Acts 21:25).[1]

The wider arc, from Jesus through Peter to Acts 21, is set out under Customs, Freedom and Offence in section 5.

"Didn't the Spirit tell him not to go to Jerusalem?"

Acts 21:4 says the disciples at Tyre "told Paul through the Spirit not to go up to Jerusalem."[1] Later, Agabus warns him in words that describe what would happen there:

"So shall the Jews at Jerusalem bind the man who owns this belt, and deliver him into the hands of the Gentiles."

Acts 21:11[1]

Readers differ here. Some take Acts 21:4 as a clear instruction that Paul disobeyed. Others read the plea as the disciples' own application of a true warning. They see Paul choosing to go on. Paul's answer in verse 13 shows that he had counted the cost. Whichever reading you take, the vow in the temple is not the point where Paul turns from grace.

"Doesn't Galatians 5:4 forbid this?"

Galatians 5:4 is the sharpest verse on this question:

"you who attempt to be justified by law; you have fallen from grace."

Galatians 5:4[1]

Galatians 2:16 gives the principle: a man is not justified by the works of the law but by faith in Jesus Christ. The danger Paul names in verse 4 is trust. The question is what a person is trusting in to be made right with God. Paul's part in the vow in Jerusalem is not described as an attempt to be justified. The chapter gives no hint that he trusted the vow for his standing before God. A believer can keep a custom for the sake of other believers without trusting it to save. That is the line this page keeps returning to.

"Doesn't the sin offering deny the cross?"

Numbers 6:14 includes a sin offering in the close of the vow. The sacrifices of the law pointed forward to Christ. Hebrews says we are made holy "through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all" (Hebrews 10:10).[1]

"Isn't this what Paul rebuked Peter for?"

In Galatians 2 Paul did oppose Peter, because Peter withdrew from Gentile believers "fearing those who were of the circumcision" (Galatians 2:12).[1] Paul's objection was to forcing Gentiles to live as Jews: "why do you compel Gentiles to live as Jews?" (Galatians 2:14).[1] In Acts 21:19–26 no Gentile was asked to change anything. Paul's own practice was his choice. The wider pattern of custom and offence is set out under Paul's Firm Line in Galatians.

"What did Paul say about the visit later?"

Years later, before Felix, Paul described the visit: "I came to bring alms and offerings to my nation". Some Jews from Asia "found me purified in the temple, neither with a mob nor with tumult" (Acts 24:17–18).[1] He called it service to his nation, not a return to the law for salvation.

The word for law here is nomos. Blue Letter Bible's concordance lists it for the law of Moses, as in Luke 2:22. It is also used for the law and the prophets as a whole, as in Matthew 22:40[6].

The Greek word can name the whole Law that Moses gave. Paul's letters go further. They separate keeping the law as a way to be right with God from keeping Jewish practices as a matter of daily life. Acts 21:19–26 sits on the side of custom. Galatians sits on the side of salvation. Seeing that line makes the chapter easier to read.

Paul's teaching on how a person is made right with God is clear. These three verses are the foundation for the whole question, in the New King James Version:

"Therefore we conclude that a man is justified by faith apart from the deeds of the law."

Romans 3:28[1]

"knowing that a man is not justified by the works of the law but by faith in Jesus Christ"

Galatians 2:16[1]

"For by grace you have been saved through faith, and that not of yourselves; it is the gift of God, not of works, lest anyone should boast."

Ephesians 2:8–9[1]

The question turns on a distinction. Being justified before God is never by the works of the law. Jewish practices are a different matter. Paul could keep some of them as a Jew, as long as nobody, himself included, treated them as the means of being made right with God. So the question to ask is not "Did Paul keep a Jewish custom?" but "Did Paul treat that custom as necessary for salvation?"

Paul's firmest line appears in Galatians. He went up to Jerusalem with Titus, a Greek. The question was whether Titus had to be circumcised. Paul did not give way:

"Yet not even Titus who was with me, being a Greek, was compelled to be circumcised."

Galatians 2:3[1]

"to whom we did not yield submission even for an hour, that the truth of the gospel might continue with you."

Galatians 2:5[1]

Paul's reason is given in the verse itself: the truth of the gospel was at stake. Later he warns that accepting circumcision as a way to be made right with God carries a heavy cost:

"Indeed I, Paul, say to you that if you become circumcised, Christ will profit you nothing."

Galatians 5:2[1]

Paul also explains the flexibility he could show in other settings:

"to the Jews I became as a Jew, that I might win Jews; to those who are under the law, as under the law, that I might win those who are under the law;"

1 Corinthians 9:20[1]

Paul's Practice and Purpose

Paul did not limit this to Jews. Here is his summary of his practice and his purpose:

"I have become all things to all men, that I might by all means save some. Now this I do for the gospel's sake, that I may be partaker of it with you."

1 Corinthians 9:22–23[1]

His practice was to adapt to the people in front of him, among Jews under the law and among others outside it. His purpose is clear. He did not let customs or differences in customs get in the way of winning people to the gospel of grace. The custom could change. The gospel did not.

So Paul could become like a Jew to reach Jews. He would not let circumcision become a requirement for Gentiles or a means of salvation. Those are two different things. The vow in Acts 21:19–26 belongs to the first. The Galatian dispute belongs to the second.

Customs, Freedom and Offence

Jesus observed customs where they were not a matter of salvation. When the temple tax collectors asked Peter whether Jesus paid, Jesus pointed out that "the sons are free" (Matthew 17:26). Even so, he paid, "lest we offend them" (Matthew 17:27).[1]

Paul told the Corinthians, "Imitate me, just as I also imitate Christ" (1 Corinthians 11:1).[1] His own practice followed the same line. Peter's failure at Antioch was about that line. He ate with Gentiles, then withdrew, "fearing those who were of the circumcision" (Galatians 2:12).[1] Paul's objection was not to the custom. It was to what the withdrawal said: that Gentiles had to live as Jews to be fully accepted (Galatians 2:14).[1]

Acts 21 fits that pattern. The vow is kept among Jews. Luke gives the motive: the elders wanted Paul to show "that those things of which they were informed concerning you are nothing" (Acts 21:24).[1] Gentile believers are told only what the earlier decree already said (Acts 21:25).[1]

Custom observed freely, to avoid offence, is not the gospel. Custom made into a condition or a marker of who belongs is where Paul stood firm (Galatians 2:3, 2:5).[1] These passages support the reading. They do not settle it alone. The wider case rests on Paul's teaching of the gospel. Galatians 5:2 shows the other side of the line: accepting circumcision as a way to be right with God is the problem, not the custom itself.[1]

Romans 14 gives the principle for matters of custom, where believers may differ. Paul writes that each should "be fully convinced in his own mind" (Romans 14:5)[1].

Colossians 2:16–17 calls the festivals, new moons and sabbaths "a shadow of things to come", with the substance found in Christ[1]. Gangel links the vow to Paul's own rule: "such behavior would not have violated Paul's principles of grace-living"[3].

The lesson is not that believers should go back under the law. It is that customs can be given up or kept for love's sake. They are never to be placed where Christ belongs. Paul's call to freedom still holds: "Stand fast therefore in the liberty by which Christ has made us free" (Galatians 5:1)[1].

Going deeper

What would change if you held the customs you keep for the sake of others?

Christians differ on Acts 21:19–26. Some see Paul's part in the vow as a mistake. Others see it as a wise act of accommodation for Jewish believers. Both views take the text seriously. Stott observes that "commentators are not agreed about what James had in mind".[3] Romans 14 asks each side to be fully convinced in their own mind (Romans 14:5)[1] and not to judge the other (Romans 14:3, 10).[1] That is a good place to start when the views differ.

Plain Response

"No, Paul did not go back on grace. Nobody is justified by the works of the law (Romans 3:28; Galatians 2:16). In Acts 21:19–26 he joined a Jewish purification to show Jewish believers that he was not against their customs. His own rule was to become "as a Jew" to win Jews (1 Corinthians 9:20). He did not treat that custom as a way to be right with God. The elders reaffirmed that Gentile believers were not to be placed under the law (Acts 21:25). Paul had already held firm that Titus was not compelled to be circumcised (Galatians 2:3). The plan did not work. Paul was arrested in the temple. Paul gave way in custom for the sake of others. He never gave way on how people are made right with God (Galatians 2:5; 5:4)."
